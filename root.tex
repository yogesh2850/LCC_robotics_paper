\begin{abstract}
Robotic inspection of an attached leaf requires more than a stable grasp: contact must
produce a useful and measurable change in the blade's pose. We study this distinction in
a volumetric finite-element maize simulation using a mobile Kinova Gen3 with either a
Robotiq 2F-85 or a Shadow Dexterous Hand. Both systems navigate, select RGB-D leaf
patches, establish force-regulated contact, and execute lift-and-roll presentations.
We distinguish controller completion, leaf response, and measurement availability rather
than treating a completed gripper sweep as successful leaf presentation. An audit of 32
archived interaction runs covers 89 attempted leaves and 65 controller completions; the
saved terminal patch-normal changes do not establish peak response or closed-loop pose
tracking, and no wrist-normal swing measurements are stored in those runs. We correct a
mixed-force aggregation and report the historical interaction matrix as exploratory,
not as causal design rules. A supporting illumination study finds that calibration with a
remote platform reference can worsen colour estimation, while a nearby reference can
reduce variation when visible. These observations motivate response-aware presentation:
estimate the local action-to-leaf response before choosing to continue, regrasp, or release.
The adaptive extension is specified separately and has not yet been evaluated. All
reported results are simulation-only; no tissue-safety or field-performance claim is made.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Chlorophyll content, and with it nitrogen status, changes leaf colour long before yield is
affected, and hand-held meters such as the SPAD-502 exploit this~\cite{markwell1995}.
Cameras could make the measurement continuous and cheap, but a leaf imaged in the field is
lit by a mixture of direct sun and sky, seen at an uncontrolled angle, partly shadowed and
often occluded by its neighbours. Image-based chlorophyll estimation therefore relies on
careful radiometric calibration~\cite{wang2014} and still degrades away from the
conditions it was fitted for.

Contact can change the geometry available to a sensor, but this is not itself a new
capability. Prior systems clamp leaves for phenotyping~\cite{atefi2019}, actively choose
views for leaf probing~\cite{foix2018}, grasp leaves for proximal hyperspectral
sensing~\cite{mortazavi2026}, and manipulate occluding leaves to reveal
fruit~\cite{yao2025}. The specific issue here is the difference between holding an
attached blade and controlling the presentation of a selected region on that blade.
A loaded jaw and completed tool rotation do not establish the region's orientation,
visibility, or contact mode. Conversely, a small terminal rotation after a return sweep
does not establish that the leaf failed to rotate earlier.

This paper asks a specific question: \emph{when a robot pinches a leaf blade and turns its
gripper, what decides whether the blade follows, and which end-effector should do it?} We
investigate it through archived simulations and separate existing evidence from the
adaptive controller needed to answer it causally. The present contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a mobile manipulation loop (RGB-D navigation and leaf targeting, canopy-aware transit
planning, force-regulated pinch, lift and $\pm35^\circ$ roll with the camera decoupled from
the roll) that runs end to end with a parallel-jaw gripper and with a 24-DoF dexterous hand;
\item a formulation separating contact, actual leaf presentation, and measurement success,
with an audit of interaction outcomes, force-setting confounds, and rotation observability;
\item a failure taxonomy over 24 plant instances (orientation, stiffness, colour
assignment) that distinguishes targeting failures from contact and measurement failures;
and
\item a supporting illumination study with a gripper-mounted colour reference (white and
grey chips on the upper jaw): the same leaf at six stages of the interaction under 19 recorded sun/sky
conditions, reporting calibration error together with measurement availability rather than
assuming that presentation improves colour accuracy.
\end{itemize}

All results are in simulation. We state this plainly because it shapes what can be
claimed: these are observations on one plant asset, not universal end-effector rankings
or field performance. A proposed response-aware extension and its falsifiable evaluation
protocol accompany this working draft; no adaptive-controller improvement is claimed yet.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Plant phenotyping robots.} The Robotanist~\cite{muellersim2017} established the
pattern of a row-following ground platform with a manipulator for contact measurements in
sorghum. Atefi et al.~\cite{atefi2019} clamp maize and sorghum leaves with a sensorised
gripper to read chlorophyll and temperature in vivo. Ahlin et al.~\cite{ahlin2016} pick
leaves with a manipulator using learned detection and visual servoing. Foix et
al.~\cite{foix2018} select informative views for leaf probing. RoMu4o~\cite{mortazavi2026}
combines leaf grasping with proximal hyperspectral sensing and local illumination.
These precedents rule out claiming leaf grasping for sensing as the novelty.

\textbf{Interaction for visibility.} Yao et al.~\cite{yao2025} plan leaf manipulation
using a perception-driven deformation graph to expose occluded fruits while accounting
for deformation. Our task concerns the pose and measurability of the grasped leaf itself.
This task difference alone is not an algorithmic contribution: a stronger method must
demonstrate that online response measurements improve presentation beyond an open-loop
sweep and a conventional visual-feedback baseline.

\textbf{Colour references.} Reference charts~\cite{mccamy1976} are standard in imaging
phenotyping, but they are usually placed in the scene by hand and imaged separately from
the plant. Mounting the board on the platform and observing it continuously with its own
camera supplies a reference measurement, but not necessarily the illuminant incident on
a shaded leaf. We evaluate rather than assume the benefit of co-location.

\textbf{Deformable manipulation and response estimation.} Online mappings between tool
motion and observed deformation are established, including Gaussian-process visual
servoing~\cite{hu2017}. Agricultural slip feedback also predates this
work~\cite{zhou2022}; low leaf response alone must not be labelled slip. Recent workshop
research estimates relative plant compliance from wind-excited video~\cite{bhowmik2026}.
Our candidate extension concerns whether a particular attached-leaf grasp can achieve a
sensing pose under a force budget, not the first deformation estimator. Real leaves have
directional tissue structure; this volumetric FEM with a scalar modulus is not a validated
anisotropic shell model. We use Isaac Lab~\cite{mittal2023} and the existing maize
FEM~\cite{plantrepo,physx} without modifying the plant for the proposed comparison.

\textbf{Row navigation.} Camera-based row following is well studied~\cite{bakker2011}; our
navigation is deliberately simple (nearest leaf cluster in the lane) because the
contribution lies elsewhere.

\section{Problem: Contact, Presentation, and Measurement}
Let $q_g$ be tool roll and $\mathbf n_t$ the normal of a tracked free-blade region,
expressed in a fixed world frame. This region must lie outside the jaw contact and retain
its identity over time. For an unoriented plane normal, define
\begin{equation}
 e_n(t)=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\left|\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf n^\star\right|\right).
\end{equation}
This metric cannot distinguish the two leaf faces; doing so requires additional temporal
or appearance evidence. We separate (i) bilateral contact within a predefined force
budget, (ii) a requested leaf pose held within an angular tolerance, and (iii) an
unoccluded, valid sensing region. Operational presentation success requires all three
simultaneously for a specified dwell. A commanded roll, stable force, or terminal
deformation alone is insufficient. Force limits are operational constraints, not
validated bounds on tissue damage.

The existing sweep controller does not implement this pose-based termination criterion.
Its archived complete outcome is called \emph{controller completion} throughout.
Future comparisons must log synchronized tool motion, regional leaf normals,
normal-estimation validity, and pad loads. Missing normals are unavailable measurements,
not zero deformation. The accompanying response-aware method proposal builds on this
formulation but is not included among the measured results.

\section{System}

\subsection{Platform and arm}
A wheeled platform (kinematic in simulation, holonomic) carries a pedestal with a Kinova
Gen3 7-DoF arm and, on the opposite side, a colour board on a post with a 26\,mm camera on a
mast 26\,cm above it. The board leans $35^\circ$ so that its face normal points straight
up when parked; its six patches are white, grey and four leaf shades from dark green to
yellow.

\subsection{Rotary tool joint and cameras}
The Robotiq 2F-85 gripper hangs from a bracket on the flange through an additional revolute
joint about the approach axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, bottom right). The 7-DoF inverse
kinematics positions the \emph{bracket}; the extra joint turns only the gripper. The wrist
RGB-D camera (14\,mm, $960{\times}540$) is mounted on the bracket 13\,cm above the pads and
8\,cm to the side, so the blade beside the pads fills the frame and the view does not move
when the gripper rolls. Two earlier designs failed: using the arm's seventh joint as the
roll with the camera on the preceding link stalled the IK against joint limits, and a
null-space bias could carry only a third of the roll because the arm's natural wrist angle
differs by $105^\circ$ between leaves at different heights. A forward-looking navigation
camera (10.5\,mm, pitched $30^\circ$ down) and a scene camera (16\,mm) sit on the mast.

\subsection{Two end-effectors}
The same bracket, tool joint and controller carry either a Robotiq 2F-85 parallel-jaw
gripper or a Shadow Dexterous Hand (24 joints, 20 actuated). The hand cannot reproduce the
gripper's motion: its thumb tip only reaches the region near the knuckles, never a straight
finger, so it forms a \emph{fingertip jaw} instead: the index is curled (MCP $1.1$, PIP
$0.5$\,rad), the other fingers curl into the palm, and the thumb closes onto the index
distal pad by first swinging its base in and then flexing its two distal joints, giving a
6.1\,cm opening that the controller treats as a virtual gripper frame (closing axis from
the index pad to the open thumb pad, mouth from the wrist toward the jaw). Its forearm puts
the flange bracket 38\,cm behind the pinch point, which the platform absorbs by parking
25\,cm further from the plant and the planner by choosing an approach heading per leaf
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{Gripper-mounted colour reference}\label{sec:chips}
Two matte chips, white (0.90) and grey (0.45) in nominal sRGB like the board's neutral
patches, $11{\times}11$\,mm, sit on the dorsal side of the upper jaw (thumb tip on the hand,
outer face of the upper finger on the gripper), 1--2\,cm from the pinched blade and facing
the wrist camera. Their only purpose is calibration: they see the light the presented blade
sees, from the same camera, in the same frame.

\subsection{Fingertips}
Each inner finger carries a 2\,g load-cell pad ($40{\times}3{\times}26$\,mm) on a fixed joint
whose incoming joint force is the contact load, faced with a rubber physics material
($\mu_s=1.6$) and a 20\,mm visual rubber block. The blocks matter for interpretation: the
plant's collision envelope is about 4\,cm thick around a blade rendered a few millimetres
thick (Sec.~\ref{sec:plant}), so the load-cell faces stop 50\,mm apart while the rendered
blade sits in the middle.

\subsection{Plant and field}\label{sec:plant}
The target is a half-scale maize plant (stalk, 14 leaves, ear) cooked as hexahedral FEM
bodies at resolution 30, 128 solver iterations, 60\,Hz, following~\cite{plantrepo}; the
recipe only stands with a 3\,kg stalk mass override and runtime damping, which we keep
unchanged. Leaves carry five nominal sRGB shades as ground truth. The field has 314
visual-only copies in agronomic rows (0.75\,m between rows, 0.35\,m within a row); the target
is the first-row plant and the robot approaches across the open headland.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Navigation}
While driving, the navigation camera is read at 3\,Hz. Leaf-coloured pixels
(hue $38$--$135^\circ$, saturation $>0.28$, value $>0.12$) are back-projected through the
depth image with the camera pose that follows from the platform's odometry and the fixed
mount, expressed in the platform frame, and restricted to the lane ahead ($|y|\le0.35$\,m,
$0.1$--$4.5$\,m out, $0.3$--$1.5$\,m up). The nearest 70\,cm-deep cluster along the row is the
plant and its median $xy$ the goal. Two rules were needed: once the plant is within 1\,m the
target is locked and estimates jumping more than 0.4\,m are rejected (otherwise, when the
plant leaves the near-range window, the ``nearest cluster'' becomes the next plant and the
platform drives through the target), and under 0.5\,m the estimate is frozen and odometry
finishes the approach (leaf tips reaching toward the camera bias the median by 20\,cm at
close range). The platform follows a decelerating profile in $x$ and a proportional lateral
correction until the plant sits at the origin of its frame within 3\,cm.

\subsection{Leaf targeting}
After arrival the scene camera provides one RGB-D frame. Rather than segmenting the image
(overlapping leaves merge into a single blob), the leaf point cloud is scanned directly:
every occupied 3\,cm voxel seeds a 4\,cm plane fit giving a patch centre (top surface) and
normal; the near-plane points within 12\,cm give the local blade axis, extent and droop
toward the stalk; clearance is counted in 1\,cm voxels against all other leaf points inside
the descent corridor, the sweep slab and the column above the patch. A patch is usable if
its normal is within $57^\circ$ of vertical, the blade extends away from the arm's side, the
patch and the pre-pinch point are within reach, droop is under 6\,cm, and fewer than 60
voxels obstruct. Blades must root within 18\,cm of the plant position found by navigation,
which excludes the in-row neighbours. Candidates are ranked by a score that rewards blade
length, distance out toward the tip and proximity, and up to $N$ targets are picked with
hues at least $10^\circ$ apart. At the pre-pinch point the wrist camera re-fits the patch
from close up (typical shift 8\,mm). Simulator nodes are used afterwards only to identify
the nearest true leaf and grade colour and deflection.

\subsection{Reach and canopy-aware transit}
Each leaf gets its own approach heading: the 24 horizontal headings that do not run over the
stalk end of the blade are ranked by how much foliage (from the RGB-D cloud, own blade
excluded) lies in the descent column and the diagonal advance slab, then by how far the
heading points away from the arm base, and the first heading for which a global inverse
kinematics of the arm (a product-of-exponentials model identified from the simulator's
articulation to 0.05\,mm) finds joint configurations inside the joint limits for both the
pinch pose and the transit pose is taken. Transits between leaves are chains of IK
waypoints: rise vertically to 14\,cm above the highest leaf point seen within 25\,cm of the
path (all plants, not only the target), traverse in 15\,cm steps, descend; each waypoint is
seeded from the previous one so the joint-space moves stay near the straight segments.
Before this, the hand swept through the target's own canopy (9\,N on the pad in transit)
and through the visual-only neighbours.

\subsection{Pinch, centring and presentation}
The gripper transits above the pre-pinch point (10\,cm from the patch on the approach side,
12\,cm up), descends, and slides across the blade with the pads open until they straddle
the patch. Closure is commanded as a normalised finger-joint target: it snaps to the gap
known from earlier holds, then creeps at $0.2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ until the filtered pad load
reaches 0.15\,N, dwells 0.35\,s, and regulates the summed load to the target
(3--4\,N in the reported runs; 2--6\,N or 2.5--7\,N hold window; 7--8\,N trip). Because
closure is symmetric about the gripper axis but the blade surface estimate is coarse, one
pad always touches first; a centring admittance moves the whole gripper along the blade
normal toward the loaded pad at up to 1\,cm/s until both pads share the load. The hold then
lifts the blade up to 30\,mm and sweeps the roll $+35^\circ\to-35^\circ\to0$ at $8^\circ$/s,
both force-limited with hysteresis so a transient does not end the motion. A leaf is
complete after six seconds of continuous valid hold with at least three valid colour frames
and the finished sweep.

\subsection{Colour}
At 1\,Hz the wrist camera samples the blade 4--8\,cm from the pads (following the lifted
blade) and the board camera samples the six patches; samples are validated by projected
depth and, for the leaf, by the hue gate. A two-point white/grey calibration is chosen by
the residual on the colour patches, and the verdict is the swatch nearest in chromaticity,
reported with a separation margin; margins below 0.15 are flagged ambiguous. Only frames
from the current uninterrupted hold count.

\subsection{Illumination study protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
The study asks how the colour estimate of one leaf changes with illumination at each stage
of the interaction. Nineteen recorded conditions (18 alternatives plus the default) are applied at run time to the sun (a distant light) and the sky (a
dome light), with illuminant chromaticities spanning field daylight (linear RGB
multipliers relative to D65): clear sky, sun elevation $20/40/60^\circ$ $\times$ azimuth
$0/90/180/270^\circ$, sun $(1.00,0.93,0.85)$ and blue sky $(0.62,0.78,1.00)$; low sun
$15^\circ$ at two azimuths, golden $(1.00,0.62,0.32)$ against a bluer sky at half
intensity; overcast, sky only, cool $(0.82,0.90,1.00)$; bright, $1.6\times$ sun; an
under-canopy condition with a green ambient $(0.72,1.00,0.55)$ standing in for light
transmitted through the leaves above; and one neutral white sun/sky; plus the neutral
condition the pipeline is tuned in. At
six stages, physics is paused, each condition is rendered and the leaf is sampled:
\emph{passive-scene} (platform RGB-D camera, after targeting), \emph{passive-wrist} (wrist
camera at the pre-pinch pose, no contact), \emph{active-pinch} (pinched, no lift, roll 0),
and \emph{active-roll} at $0$, $+35$ and $-35^\circ$ after the lift. Every sample yields
three estimates: raw chromaticity, board-calibrated (two-point white/grey from the platform
board seen by its own camera) and chip-calibrated (illuminant gain from the white chip
when usable, otherwise grey; archived samples are recomputed with a common calibration
in the analysis). Ground truth is the material of the leaf under the sampled pixel (semantic
segmentation), so the error is the chromaticity distance to the true shade and the class
verdict is the nearest of four non-neutral chart swatches. Five material shades are
mapped to those four swatch classes; this is not five-class shade recognition.

\section{Experiments}

All runs use Isaac Sim 6.0 / Isaac Lab~3 on one RTX~5090, 60\,Hz physics, 30\,Hz control,
headless with all cameras rendering; a two-leaf run takes 6--8\,min of wall time.

\subsection{Navigation}
From a start 2.5\,m behind and 0.25\,m beside the goal, the plant estimate in the odometry
frame stayed within 17\,mm of the true stalk from the first frame at 2.5\,m range, locked at
0.96\,m, and the platform parked at $(-1.1, +1.5)$\,cm from nominal in 6.1\,s (14 camera
updates). The same numbers reproduced across all later runs
($\le1.3$\,cm, $\le1.5$\,cm).

\subsection{Leaf targeting}
On the scene frame after arrival the scan produced 12 patch candidates of which 4 were
usable; the two chosen landed 3\,mm from the FEM leaves that the ground-truth targeting had
picked in earlier runs (Fig.~\ref{fig:detect}). With in-row neighbours 0.35\,m away and the
root-at-plant filter, this nominal example selected the target plant. This does not
establish cross-orientation identity accuracy: the broader matrix includes neighbour targets.

\subsection{Manipulation across plant instances and end-effectors}\label{sec:manip}
Table~\ref{tab:manip} reports the outcomes of the study matrix: 12 plant instances (yaw
$0/120/240^\circ$, leaf modulus $3{\times}10^4$ and $3{\times}10^5$\,Pa, two shade
assignments), up to three hue-distinct leaves each, for both end-effectors, all with
autonomous drive-in, RGB-D targeting and canopy-aware transits. With the Shadow Hand every
leaf of the four instances at the tuned orientation completed (12/12, pinch
$2.0{\pm}0.2$\,N, hold 20\,s, sweep $\pm35^\circ$, no force trip), whereas the eight instances
turned by $120^\circ$ and $240^\circ$ exposed the targeting rather than the manipulation:
three runs found no patch that passed the reach and clearance filters, five targets were
\emph{ghost} patches on visual-only neighbour leaves (the jaw closed on nothing), two holds
never had a visible sample point, one approach stalled. Of the 19 leaves that were real and
reachable, 16 were pinched and presented. Leaf stiffness ($3{\times}10^4$ vs
$3{\times}10^5$\,Pa) did not change the completion rate (8 vs 8) but did change the turn: the
blade followed the $\pm35^\circ$ roll by up to $7^\circ$ at the softer modulus and by
$0$--$2^\circ$ at the stiffer one, consistent with the torque budget of Sec.~\ref{sec:turn}.
The 2F-85 shows the same structure at a higher pinch force (4\,N): 8/10 leaves at the tuned
orientation, none at $120^\circ$ (four runs without a usable patch), 2 of 9 at $240^\circ$
(six ghost targets), i.e.\ the generalisation limit is the targeting stage, common to both
end-effectors, not the hand.

Two further iterations on the eight turned instances (Shadow Hand) locate the limit more
precisely. Dropping the ``blade points away from the arm'' filter (obsolete once the heading
is chosen per leaf) and tightening the root-at-plant radius produced targets at $120^\circ$
but not fewer ghosts (6 of 16). Adding the planting grid as a prior, i.e.\ requiring the blade
to root closer to the target than to any neighbour, removed ghosts entirely (0 of 10) at the
price of fewer candidates. What remains is a hold that our own validity rule rejects: in 6 of
10 leaves the thumb pressed the blade against the finger body rather than the instrumented
index pad, so the lower load cell read under 0.08\,N while the summed load (1.2--1.8\,N),
the lift (30\,mm) and 55--59 depth-validated colour frames were all present. The leaf was
presented and measured; the pipeline did not believe it. Whether to count a one-sided hold
is a design choice we leave explicit rather than hide in a threshold.

\subsection{Exploratory interaction study and evidence audit}\label{sec:interaction}
The archived interaction set contains 17 Robotiq runs (47 attempted leaves, 34 controller
completions) and 15 Shadow runs (42 attempts, 31 completions). These attempts share one
plant geometry and are not independent biological replicates. Current code supports
wrist-normal measurements, but none of these 89 archived attempts contains a stored
\texttt{sensor\_swing\_deg} measurement. The logged \texttt{blade\_turn\_deg} is the
patch-normal difference at leaf completion or timeout, potentially after returning tool
roll to zero. It is not peak turn or synchronized roll tracking. The matrix is therefore
descriptive evidence of configuration-dependent outcomes, not proof that the leaf
followed, slipped, or failed to move during the sweep.

The original aggregation pooled the 2 and 6\,N force runs into the 4\,N, fraction-0.55,
soft-leaf cell. Separating force settings changes its completion count from 8/9 to 2/3
and its mean terminal angle from $7.4^\circ$ to $10.5^\circ$. The paper-side audit script
records source hashes, per-attempt metadata, missing observations, and strict cell
denominators without changing simulator code or the plant.

The historical matrix varies camera-selected pinch fraction (0.35, 0.55, 0.75),
leaf modulus ($3{\times}10^4$, $3{\times}10^5$, $3{\times}10^6$\,Pa), Robotiq target force
(2, 4, 6\,N), and placement offset ($\pm1.5/\pm3$\,cm normal to the blade and $\pm2$\,cm
across it). These settings have already been run; no new plant modifications are proposed.
Table~\ref{tab:interaction} reports the archived outcomes with the force pooling corrected.
At the soft modulus, the nominal-force Robotiq mid-blade and outer cells each complete
2/3 attempts. At fraction 0.55 the mean terminal angles at 2, 4 and 6\,N are
$3.0^\circ$, $10.5^\circ$ and $8.5^\circ$: not a monotonic force-response relation.
Selected leaf identities also differ across cells, precluding a matched causal estimate.
The Shadow mid-blade and outer cells show larger soft-leaf terminal changes, but different
force budgets, selected leaves and hand geometry prevent attributing this to thumb
compliance or ranking the hands universally.

Placement-error runs demonstrate recovery on some tested targets, not a guaranteed
capture range. Camera-selected patches and reachable leaves must be paired before
comparing sensitivity. The legacy normal metric also folds to $[0,90^\circ]$ and removes
values above $60^\circ$ (one attempt per hand). Such values may reflect normal flips,
buckling, or correspondence errors; being larger than tool roll is not itself a valid
reason to reject them. Our audit retains raw and folded values and flags these exclusions.
A prospective estimator must gate on fit and tracking quality instead.

\subsection{Supporting study: colour under illumination}\label{sec:colour}
Fig.~\ref{fig:strip} shows the six measurement stages of one leaf, Fig.~\ref{fig:methods}
and Table~\ref{tab:colour} the study over the Shadow matrix (225--433 valid samples per stage,
15--23 leaves); the 2F-85 matrix (190--398 samples per stage, 10--21 leaves) is reported in the supplementary tables. Together the two matrices contain 3,800
recorded rows, of which 3,555 are valid leaf samples. Calibration availability differs
between methods, so aggregate accuracies below are descriptive rather than paired causal
comparisons. Paired comparisons must intersect valid samples by run, leaf, stage and condition.

\emph{The board on the platform is the worst estimator.} Calibrating with the platform
board lowers four-swatch accuracy below the uncalibrated chromaticity at every stage
(0.46--0.57 vs 0.55--0.68) and makes the verdict the most illumination-sensitive: the error
spread over the recorded conditions of one leaf is 0.033--0.040 for the board against 0.022--0.027
raw, and 44--70\,\% of leaves change class with the light. The board sees the open sky while
the leaf sits in the canopy; a two-point calibration then moves the leaf colour the wrong way.

\emph{A reference next to the leaf gives the most stable verdict.} Chip calibration has the
smallest spread (0.016--0.022), the fewest flips (33--44\,\% of leaves) and the best accuracy
(0.62--0.70) wherever the chips are exposed like the leaf, which on the Shadow thumb is
66\,\% of valid leaf samples at roll 0 and none at roll $+35^\circ$ (the thumb turns its back to
the camera). On the paired subset chip and raw estimates are close in mean error
(0.086 vs 0.077 at the pinch); the gain is in stability, not in the mean. With the 2F-85,
whose chips face the camera at every roll, the chip flip rate falls to 13--44\,\% of leaves
at the active stages against 64--90\,\% raw and 64--75\,\% board, with the smallest spread
(0.014--0.023 vs 0.021--0.027 raw and 0.027--0.037 board).

\emph{Presentation buys identity and access, not chromaticity.} For the same leaf and
condition the chromaticity error after pinching, lifting or rolling differs from the
close-up passive view by less than 0.005. The higher accuracy of the active stages in
Table~\ref{tab:colour} (0.65--0.68 vs 0.57) is selection: the leaves that could be pinched
are the ones that were well exposed. Depth validation and evaluation-time semantic lookup establish what was sampled, not
that it belongs to the intended target; the study can grade the material under a different
leaf pixel. Nor does unsigned normal change establish that both faces were seen.
These require separate identity and face-visibility checks. A proxy view selection over
the four active presentations (the best-exposed chip) did not beat the average view.

\subsection{Mechanical interpretation and its limits}\label{sec:turn}
The visible deformation in the development videos motivates investigating action transfer,
but Table~\ref{tab:interaction} measures a terminal state rather than tracking through the
sweep. An engineering friction-torque estimate is
\begin{equation}
 |\tau_{\mathrm{contact}}| \lesssim \mu F_{\mathrm{sum}}r_{\mathrm{eff}},
\end{equation}
where $F_{\mathrm{sum}}$ sums opposing normal loads and $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ depends on the
pressure distribution. It is not generally equal to half the pad width. Tissue geometry,
anchoring, local deformation and finger compliance also affect the response. A comparison
with the torsional rigidity of a coarse collision envelope is therefore only a heuristic,
not identification of tissue stiffness or proof of sliding.

Development run rq33 showed visible deformation of a light-green leaf, with a logged
terminal normal change of $42.8^\circ$ and 21\,mm patch displacement
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}). That observation supports studying deformation-aware presentation,
not a universal torque margin or dominant pinch location. We retain the historical
plant-settling checks below as provenance. The plant asset, attachments, masses, materials,
collision resolution and damping remain fixed for the proposed future controller study.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\textbf{Simulation only, and what the renderer can and cannot say.} Every number comes from one
plant model in one simulator, with painted shades as ground truth rather than chlorophyll.
Two rendering facts shaped the study and are worth stating for anyone repeating it. First,
the real-time path tracer adds a neutral ambient fill by default; with it, illuminant
colour barely reaches the image (a red sun and sky rendered a white card almost white), so
every illumination result would have been an artefact. We keep the fill at the level the
perception was tuned for but scale and tint it with the sky in every condition, which makes it
a crude stand-in for diffuse sky light in the canopy. Second, the leaf material is nearly
Lambertian and the camera has neither noise, limited dynamic range nor automatic white
balance; the benefits a real camera would draw from lifting a leaf out of deep shade
(signal-to-noise, exposure) are therefore not captured here, and the reported gains of
presentation cannot be assumed conservative: idealized sensing, contact geometry and
illumination can bias different metrics in either direction.

\textbf{Ghost targets.} The field around the target is populated with visual-only plants.
The RGB-D targeting occasionally chooses a patch on a neighbour's leaf that has no physics;
the jaw then closes on nothing. We report these separately (ground-truth gap above 30\,mm)
because in the field the same patch would be a real, pinchable leaf of the wrong plant, which
is a targeting error of a different kind.

\textbf{Where the reference sits.} The chips are exposed like the leaf only when the upper
jaw faces the camera and the light: on the Shadow thumb they are valid in about 66\,\% of valid leaf
samples at roll 0 and never at roll $+35^\circ$, on the 2F-85 finger in about 23--95\,\% across wrist stages. A reference that
is to be trusted must be placed, and rolled, with the same care as the leaf; the number
that matters is not the calibration model but whether reference and sample share the light.

\textbf{What we believe transfers.} The force-then-centre pinch, the force-limited presentation
with the camera decoupled from the roll, the canopy-aware transit planning with a global IK,
the depth- and semantics-validated sampling, and the co-located reference are
implemented for both tools, but their hardware transfer remains untested. The failure taxonomy (unreachable posture, approach timeout,
ghost target, no usable patch, one-sided hold) is the list a field system has to close; the
planting-grid prior removed ghost targets in the tested refinement subset.

\textbf{Next.} A real-plant study with leaves of known SPAD values and a camera with noise and
auto-exposure, a learned segmenter, and the pinch location chosen explicitly for compliance.
\todo{Real-robot experiments before submission.}

\section{Conclusion}
We built and evaluated, in simulation, a mobile manipulator that navigates to a maize plant,
finds and pinches leaves from RGB-D data alone, presents them to a stationary wrist camera
under regulated force, and grades their colour against a reference board under the same
light. The nominal loop completes useful inspection sequences, but broader matrices expose
targeting, contact-observability and measurement failures. The archived rotation metric
cannot establish how faithfully a blade follows its gripper. A stronger contribution is
a response-aware presentation policy evaluated with synchronized leaf-pose feedback and
matched baselines, rather than an unsupported universal design rule. That policy remains
proposed; the existing plant is held fixed for its future evaluation.
